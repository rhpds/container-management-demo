= Centralized logging with LokiStack

== Demo flow

=== Know
Position centralized logging as the fix for the classic "the pod already restarted and the logs are gone" problem, and explain why the modern Loki-based stack fits small environments.

=== Show
Find logs from a pod that no longer exists — proving that logs outlive the containers that produced them.

== Introduction

By default, container logs in OpenShift live only as long as the pod that produced them (`oc logs` reads directly from the container runtime on the node). Once a pod is deleted, evicted, or replaced — which happens constantly in Kubernetes — its logs disappear unless something has captured them centrally.

*OpenShift Logging* solves this using three components working together:

* *Vector*: A lightweight collector that runs as a `DaemonSet`, tailing container and node logs and forwarding them.
* *Loki Operator / LokiStack*: A horizontally scalable, cost-efficient log store (no Elasticsearch cluster to babysit), queried using LogQL. Logs are organized into three default streams: `application`, `infrastructure`, and `audit`.
* *Logging UI Plugin*: Embeds a dedicated `Logs` view directly inside `Observe` in the OpenShift web console — no separate Kibana or Grafana login required.

TIP: The Loki Operator ships a purpose-built sizing profile called *`1x.demo`*. Unlike the production-oriented `1x.extra-small` and larger profiles, `1x.demo` is explicitly designed for proof-of-concept and small or single-node clusters — it is the right choice for this demo, and for any environment where you want centralized logging without dedicating significant CPU and memory to it.

[NOTE]
====
*Pre-demo requirement:* The full LokiStack stack (MinIO, Loki Operator, OpenShift Logging Operator, LokiStack instance, ClusterLogForwarder) must be deployed before this section. Allow 10–15 minutes on a fresh cluster. See xref:00-initial-setup.adoc[Initial Setup] for step-by-step instructions.
====

== Demonstration

=== Step 1: Open the Logs view

. In the *Administrator* perspective, navigate to `Observe` -> `Logs`.
. You are now looking at a centralized stream of logs from every pod on the cluster, organised into the three default streams: *application*, *infrastructure*, and *audit*.

[TIP]
====
*"On vanilla Kubernetes, there is no built-in log aggregation. You assemble your own stack — Fluentd or Fluent Bit, Elasticsearch or Loki, Kibana or Grafana — and you maintain it. On OpenShift, this view was available the moment the logging stack was deployed. No custom dashboards, no index patterns, no log pipeline YAML to maintain by hand."*
====

=== Step 2: Find logs from a deleted pod

This is the core story of centralized logging.

. In the namespace filter, select the `demo-lightspeed` project (created during the Lightspeed section of Chapter 4).
. Search for `bad-app` in the free-text search field.
. The `echo starting && exit 1` log line from the crashed pod appears — even though that pod was deleted long ago.

[TIP]
====
*"That pod no longer exists on this cluster. But its log is here. Centralized logging decouples the life of your evidence from the life of your container. In a production incident, this is the difference between having a forensic trail and having nothing."*
====

=== Step 3: Search across all namespaces

. Clear the namespace filter.
. Search for `CrashLoopBackOff` across all namespaces.
. Any pods that have recently crash-looped across the entire cluster appear in a single view — no need to know which namespace to look in, no need to `oc logs` pod by pod.

[TIP]
====
*"This is how a platform team does triage. One search, one screen, entire cluster. Compare this to logging into individual nodes or running `kubectl logs` across dozens of namespaces one at a time."*
====
